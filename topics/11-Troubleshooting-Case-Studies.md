# Topic 11: Troubleshooting & Case Studies

## Overview
Diagnosing computational prediction failures, wet-lab validation mismatches, and structured problem-solving for common real-world AI vaccine design scenarios.

---

### Q1: Your computational pipeline predicts a set of T-cell epitopes with strong HLA binding scores and good population coverage, but experimental ELISPOT validation using donor PBMC samples shows substantially weaker-than-predicted T-cell responses across most tested epitopes. Walk through your troubleshooting approach.

**A:**
**Systematic troubleshooting framework:**
1. **Distinguish binding prediction failure from downstream immunogenicity-step failure (connecting to Topic 03's multi-step model):** Since MHC binding prediction is only one of several biological steps determining actual immunogenicity (Topic 03, Q2 — processing, TCR repertoire recognition, tolerance), first assess whether the issue is likely at the binding step itself (e.g., by checking in vitro binding assay data if available, distinguishing predicted-binding-but-not-confirmed-binding from confirmed-binding-but-not-immunogenic) versus a downstream step — this significantly narrows where to focus further investigation
2. **Check donor panel HLA typing and representativeness against the population coverage analysis assumptions:** Verify that the actual donor panel used for ELISPOT validation genuinely represents the HLA alleles the computational population coverage analysis was designed around — a mismatch between the intended target HLA alleles and the actual tested donor panel's HLA types (e.g., if donor recruitment inadvertently skewed toward different alleles than assumed) could produce an apparent prediction failure that's actually a donor panel representativeness issue rather than a genuine computational prediction error
3. **Assess prior antigen exposure history across the donor panel:** T-cell response magnitude in ELISPOT assays depends heavily on donors' prior exposure history to the specific antigen/pathogen (naive versus previously exposed/vaccinated donors would be expected to show quite different baseline response characteristics) — if the donor panel's exposure history wasn't adequately characterized or controlled for, apparent "weak" responses might partly reflect a predominantly naive donor panel rather than a genuine prediction failure, an important distinction requiring donor metadata review
4. **Review assay technical parameters and positive/negative control performance:** Before concluding the computational predictions themselves are flawed, verify the ELISPOT assay's technical execution was sound (appropriate positive control responses confirming assay sensitivity, appropriate negative control background levels) — technical assay issues (e.g., suboptimal peptide concentration, PBMC viability issues) are a mundane but common explanation for weaker-than-expected results across an entire experimental batch, and should be ruled out before attributing the discrepancy to computational prediction error
5. **If the above are ruled out, revisit the processing/presentation prediction components specifically:** If binding predictions are independently confirmed accurate (via in vitro binding data) and assay/donor panel factors are ruled out, the remaining likely explanation is a gap in the processing/presentation prediction steps (proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport) rather than binding prediction itself — this should prompt specific review and potential refinement of the processing prediction components of the pipeline (Topic 03, Q2), rather than concluding that MHC binding prediction broadly is unreliable when the issue may be specifically localized to a different pipeline component

### Q2: Case study — A structure-based stabilized antigen design shows excellent predicted stability and immunogenicity in computational modeling, but experimental expression yields are extremely low, making the design impractical to advance despite its promising immunological profile. How do you approach this trade-off and inform the path forward?

**A:**
**Systematic diagnostic and resolution approach:**
1. **Characterize the specific expression failure mode before assuming a fundamental design incompatibility:** Determine whether the low yield reflects a fundamental structural/stability problem in the specific expression system used (e.g., the engineered stabilizing mutations creating folding difficulties in that particular production cell line) versus a more addressable expression-system-specific issue (e.g., codon usage optimization gaps, suboptimal signal peptide choice, or other expression-construct-level factors distinct from the core antigen structural design) — this distinction determines whether the path forward requires revisiting the core structural design or can be addressed through expression construct/system optimization alone
2. **Assess whether alternative expression systems might resolve the yield issue without requiring structural redesign:** Given that different expression systems (various mammalian cell lines, insect cell systems, and others) can show substantially different yield/folding success for the same antigen design, systematically evaluating whether an alternative production system resolves the yield problem is often a more efficient first step than immediately pursuing further structural redesign, particularly if the immunological profile of the current design is otherwise excellent
3. **If structural redesign is genuinely needed, use computational tools to search for stability-yield trade-off resolution, not just re-optimize for stability alone:** If expression system alternatives don't resolve the issue and structural redesign is warranted, the computational redesign process (Topic 02) should explicitly incorporate expression/manufacturability prediction alongside stability/immunogenicity optimization (connecting to Topic 05's manufacturability co-design principle) — searching for alternative stabilizing mutation combinations or design variants that maintain strong immunological properties while improving expression compatibility, rather than treating the original highest-stability design as the only acceptable option and simply abandoning the antigen candidate as impractical
4. **Make an explicit, documented trade-off decision if a perfect solution isn't found, rather than defaulting to either extreme without genuine analysis:** If genuine tension remains between immunological optimality and practical manufacturability after reasonable optimization effort, explicitly document the trade-off analysis and the resulting decision (e.g., accepting a somewhat less immunologically optimal but genuinely manufacturable design, versus continuing redesign efforts, versus deprioritizing this specific antigen target in favor of an alternative candidate) — this decision should be made collaboratively with manufacturing/CMC and program leadership (Topic 10) with clear documentation of the reasoning, rather than either silently compromising on manufacturability without flagging the trade-off, or rigidly insisting on the immunologically "optimal" design regardless of practical production feasibility

---

## Summary
Rigorous troubleshooting of AI vaccine design prediction failures requires systematically distinguishing binding/structural prediction errors, downstream biological processing gaps, donor/assay technical factors, and genuine manufacturability trade-offs — resisting the temptation to attribute discrepancies to a single convenient explanation before working through the full space of plausible, evidence-supported root causes.
